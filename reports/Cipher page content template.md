# One fixed template turns ciphers into stories

Every cipher page should follow one fixed nine-part template: an always-visible "stage" (title, a standfirst of at most 16 words, the simulator in a pre-filled default state, and a short caption for the current step), followed in the reading panel by Hook, Try it, How it works, The story, How it was broken, Challenge, What came next and Further reading. The core article should run about **900 to 1,200 words** on a typical page (about 700 for a simple cipher such as Caesar, up to about 1,500 for Enigma), written at roughly US grade 8 in a direct second-person voice, with the simulator touchable within the first 100 words. Three findings drive this shape. First, most readers scroll but never click, so nothing essential can live only behind an interaction, and the stage must tell the whole story untouched. Second, learning research favours trying and predicting before the explanation, short segments, words placed next to the visual they explain, and practice with informative feedback. Third, both NN/g and Apple warn against putting long content in a bottom sheet, so on phones the panel needs three states, with the fully expanded state behaving like a page. The simulator and 3D model sit outside the panel and stay visible while reading; the panel is open by default on desktop; only optional depth (maths, wiring tables, extra history, full references, challenge solutions) is collapsed. The largest uncertainty is that almost all the evidence comes from classrooms, labs, news graphics and museums: no study tests section order, word counts or a sheet-based article beside a 3D canvas for self-directed readers, so the numbers here are editorial rules to be checked with real readers.

## The recommended template: a stage that works untouched, a panel that reads like a page

The page has two layers of text. The first is the **stage**, which is never hidden. The second is the **article**, which lives in the reading panel. The split exists because of the best-known data on interactive content: Gregor Aisch of the New York Times reported that about 85% of readers did not click a prominent button on a couple of 2015 graphics, and concluded that you "should not hide important content behind interactions" ([vis4.net](http://www.vis4.net/blog/in-defense-of-interactive-graphics/)). His colleague Archie Tse put it as a rule: "If you make the reader click or do anything other than scroll, something spectacular has to happen," and "if content is important for readers to see, don't hide it" ([Tse slides](https://github.com/archietse/malofiej-2016/blob/master/tse-malofiej-2016-slides.pdf)). On a phone the panel starts collapsed, so a reader who never opens it must still leave with the main idea. The stage does that job through a default simulator state that already shows the point (a message already typed, already enciphered, the path already lit) and a caption track of six to eight stand-alone captions, one per simulator step.

The table below is the template. Word counts are editorial targets derived from the evidence in the next section, not measured optima.

| # | Section (fixed label) | Where it sits | What it contains | Target words |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Stage | Always visible, outside the panel | Cipher name, date and place; standfirst carrying the page's single message; simulator or 3D model in an informative default state; a one-sentence imperative instruction ("Type your name"); step controls; the caption for the current step; input and output shown as plain text | Standfirst up to 16; each caption 40 to 60 |
| 1 | Hook | Panel, top | One named person, one concrete moment, the stakes. Ends on a question about the mechanism that a novice can understand but cannot answer, then points back to the simulator | 80 to 120 |
| 2 | Try it | Panel | A tiny task with a prediction first ("Type AAAA. What do you expect?"). A "Show me" link is always available | 40 to 60 |
| 3 | How it works | Panel | Two to five segments, one idea each, simplest part first, building to the whole. Each segment has a "Show me" link that sets the simulator to the matching preset. One fully worked example of a single letter, then a half-finished one for the reader | 100 to 150 per segment |
| 4 | The story | Panel | Who used it, when, and what depended on it. Only history that explains the mechanism or its weakness; the rest goes in a collapsed "More history" block | 130 to 180 |
| 5 | How it was broken | Panel | Opens with a predict-then-reveal prompt, then the method, with the reader performing one step of the attack in the simulator | 150 to 250 |
| 6 | Challenge | Panel | Three graded messages to crack (easy, medium, hard), framed as an intercept from the Hook's story, plus two or three recall questions. Feedback explains why. Optional, never a gate | 40 to 80 of prose |
| 7 | What came next | Panel | Why it mattered, the weakness that led to the next cipher, and a link to the next page in the chronology | 80 to 120 |
| 8 | Further reading | Panel, bottom | Two to four curated items; full references in a collapsed block | Short list |

**The simulator and 3D model belong on the stage, not in the article.** For a device cipher the 3D model is the stage's first view: the whole object, labelled, draggable, with an "open it up" control, which is how Bartosz Ciechanowski opens "Mechanical Watch" after about 90 words ([ciechanow.ski](https://ciechanow.ski/mechanical-watch/)). Stepping forward moves from the model to the working simulator view (keys, lamps, signal path). For a cipher with no device, the stage holds the simulator alone. One simulator shown in several preset states replaces a string of separate widgets, which keeps roughly the exemplars' ratio of one interactive state per 200 to 250 words without building thirty sets of bespoke demos.

**What is shown and what is hidden follows one rule: hide only what a reader can skip without losing the argument.** Always visible are the title, standfirst, default simulator state, instruction, current caption, step controls and a progress indicator. Inside the panel, all eight article sections appear in full, with no accordions or tabs around core text. Collapsed inside the panel are the "Dig deeper" blocks (key-space arithmetic, wiring tables, variants), "More history", full references, and challenge hints and solutions, which unlock after an attempt. Nothing that matters goes in a tooltip.

Each section carries its fixed label as a small eyebrow and a page-specific heading that states the point in sentence case, for example "How it works" above "Each key press turns a rotor, so the alphabet keeps changing". The fixed label gives series consistency, as Ciechanowski's identical closing sections do across articles ([GPS](https://ciechanow.ski/gps/)). The descriptive heading serves scanners, discussed below.

## The evidence behind each choice

### Interact within 100 words, because triggered interest fades fast

The exemplars agree on timing. Ciechanowski's "Mechanical Watch" and "GPS" reach their first interactive after about 90 and 70 words, Red Blob Games' A* introduction after about 35, and "Parable of the Polygons" after about 110, each with a one-sentence instruction directly above the interactive ([Red Blob Games](https://www.redblobgames.com/pathfinding/a-star/introduction.html); [ncase.me](https://ncase.me/polygons/)). Interest research explains why this works. In Hidi and Renninger's four-phase model, triggered situational interest "may fizzle as quickly as it is triggered" unless something sustains it ([InformalScience summary](https://www.informalscience.org/four-phase-model-interest-development)). Loewenstein describes curiosity as a felt gap in knowledge that is intense but transient ([abstract record](https://stafforini.com/works/loewenstein-1994-psychology-curiosity-review/)). The Hook therefore opens a specific gap about the mechanism and does not close it; the simulator and "How it works" close it, and each segment ends by opening a smaller one.

The Hook is a story because narrative text is understood and remembered better than exposition: a meta-analysis of 33,078 participants found **g = .55 overall and g = .72 for memory** ([Mar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577/)). The same paper warns of very high heterogeneity and publication bias, and its authors caution against forcing all information into narrative, so the template keeps the story short and uses plain explanation for the mechanism.

### Predict and try first, explain second, then practise with feedback

"Try it" comes before "How it works" because problem-solving before instruction beats the reverse order for conceptual understanding, with a moderate effect across 53 studies ([Sinha and Kapur 2021](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4KnMgZq7)). Asking for a prediction raises curiosity and improves memory for the correct answer ([Brod and Breitwieser 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41539-019-0056-y)), and readers asked to predict before data is revealed recall it better ([Distill](https://distill.pub/2020/communicating-with-interactive-articles/)). Two limits shape the design. Pretesting gains depend on seeing the correct answer afterwards ([Pan and Carpenter 2023](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PR_2023.pdf)) and are confined to the items pretested ([Plymouth](https://researchportal.plymouth.ac.uk/en/publications/pre-testing-effects-are-target-specific-and-are-not-driven-by-a-g-2/)), so prompts target the two or three properties the page wants remembered. And the Sinha and Kapur analysis found younger learners did better with instruction first, so the task stays tiny and "Show me" is always one tap away.

The worked example in "How it works" rests on a meta-analysis giving **g = 0.48** for worked examples, which also found that correct examples alone beat mixes with incorrect ones and that adding self-explanation prompts was associated with smaller effects ([Barbieri et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1.pdf)). That evidence is from mathematics; its transfer to tracing a letter through a cipher is plausible but untested. The Challenge rests on the testing effect, where practice tests beat comparison conditions at **g = 0.61 to 0.70** ([Adesope et al. 2017](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/)), and on feedback research showing an overall d = 0.48 that depends substantially on how much information the feedback carries ([Wisniewski et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full)). A wrong answer therefore shows the correct path through the machine, not a red cross.

### History goes after the mechanism, because off-topic colour costs novices most

The riskiest content on these pages is the most tempting: vivid anecdotes. A meta-analysis of seductive details, covering 58 studies and more than 7,500 students, found that interesting but irrelevant material lowers learning ([WSU News](https://news.wsu.edu/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/)). A secondary account of the same work reports the harm as largest in lessons under five minutes and for novices ([Learning and the Brain](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/seductive-details-when-do-cool-stories-and-videos-interfere-with-learning/)), which describes these pages exactly. The test for every anecdote and image is whether deleting it would make the mechanism harder to understand. The Hook passes only when its story is about the mechanism. Everything else waits for "The story", after explanation, or goes into "More history". The same test applies to the 3D model: it earns its place when labelled and tied to steps of the explanation, and is decoration otherwise. This is an inference; no direct evidence on 3D models for general readers was found.

This ordering also matches practice. The acclaimed explorables use history only as framing at the top and tail, while dCode buries it at the bottom of a search-driven FAQ and Crypto Museum leads with features and specifications in catalogue order ([dCode](https://www.dcode.fr/caesar-cipher); [Crypto Museum](https://www.cryptomuseum.com/crypto/enigma/i/index.htm)). No surveyed cipher site puts a visible-mechanism simulator, the human story and the cryptanalysis on one page. Cryptii's Enigma page, for example, offers about 37 words of introduction and no explanation of rotors or plugboard ([Cryptii](https://cryptii.com/pipes/enigma-machine)). That combination is this site's opening.

### Word counts come from museum labels and a phone reading study

The V&A's gallery text guide supplies the layering: intro panels of 150 to 180 words, section panels of 130 to 150, object labels of 50 to 60, a first sentence "no more than 16 words long", and every text able to stand alone because visitors read in an order the author cannot control. The guide insists that short texts increase what visitors absorb ([V&A guide](https://thegardenstrust.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/VA_Gallery-Text-Writing-Guidelines_online_Web.pdf)). Its reader model of "paddlers, swimmers and divers" maps onto the template's layers: paddlers get the stage and headings, swimmers the article, divers the collapsed blocks. Segment length follows from segmentation research, where learner-paced meaningful segments beat continuous presentation across 56 investigations ([Rey et al. 2019](https://is.tuebingen.mpg.de/publications/rey2018segmenting)).

The total length is anchored by NN/g's 2016 study of 276 participants, which found no practical comprehension penalty on phones, but found that a roughly 988-word, grade-12 article was read about 30 ms per word slower on mobile, while a roughly 404-word, grade-8 article was not ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content/)). A page of grade-8 prose in segments of 100 to 150 words stays inside the tested safe condition. The research notes produced two candidate totals, about 600 to 900 words from the learning-science reading and about 1,000 to 1,500 from the exemplar and museum reading. The recommended 900 to 1,200 sits where they overlap, well below Ciechanowski's 7,000 to 8,000 and close to "Parable of the Polygons" at about 1,300. Only "How it works" scales with the cipher; every other section keeps its budget.

### Tone: conversational, plain, and honest about doubt

Conversational second-person wording improves retention (d = 0.30) and transfer (d = 0.54) in a meta-analysis of 3,312 students ([Ginns et al. 2013](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/designing-instructional-text-in-a-conversational-style-a-meta-ana/)). California and New York City both set grade 8 or lower for public content ([California ODI](https://hub.innovation.ca.gov/content-design/principles/write-in-plain-language); [NYC Blueprint](https://blueprint.cityofnewyork.us/content/plain-language-and-readability/)), and GOV.UK's long-standing limit is 25 words per sentence ([Inside GOV.UK](https://insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk/2014/08/04/sentence-length-why-25-words-is-our-limit/)). The house voice is therefore: "you" and active verbs; sentences averaging 15 to 20 words with a 25-word ceiling; one idea per paragraph; each technical term defined once, at the moment it is highlighted on the model, since the V&A warns that an unexplained specialist word makes readers stop. Two refinements come from museum practice. Admit uncertainty where attribution is legendary, as the V&A's ninth point requires. And avoid chirpiness: label-writing juror Beverly Serrell disliked labels that over-use "we" and "you", repeat instructions or end in exclamation marks ([Museum-Ed](https://www.museum-ed.org/best-practices-in-museum-exhibition-writing-2004/)). Use "you" for instructions and observations, not for cheerleading.

## The Enigma page, filled in

The outline below applies the template. The teaching points (rotors, stepping, reflector, plugboard, a letter never enciphering to itself, daily settings and repeated message keys) are the ones the research notes identify as explanatory. **The historical beats are placeholders to be written from Singh's book and fact-checked; this research did not verify them.** Target core length is about 1,450 words, the top of the range, because Enigma needs five "How it works" segments.

| # | Section and page heading | Outline | Simulator or model state | Words |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Stage. Standfirst: "A typewriter that changed its own code with every letter, and the people who beat it." | Default: the 3D machine, lid closed, parts labelled, with HELLO already typed and its ciphertext shown as text. Instruction: "Press any key and watch which lamp lights." Eight captions, one per step | 3D model, whole machine | 15 plus 8 captions of about 50 |
| 1 | Hook: "Every morning the code started again from nothing" | One operator, one morning, setting the machine from the day's key sheet. The stake: anyone without the sheet faces an astronomically large number of settings. Closing question: how could a machine this well guarded ever be read? | Model, camera on the settings | 110 |
| 2 | Try it: "Press the same key four times" | "Type AAAA. Before you do, guess: the same letter four times, or four different ones?" Reveal: four different letters, and none of them is A. Hold both observations for later | Simulator, keys and lamps, lid closed | 50 |
| 3a | How it works: "A key closes a circuit and a lamp lights" | The machine is a battery, a keyboard and 26 lamps. Without scrambling, A would light A. Worked example begins: follow one letter | Lid open, signal path lit, rotors bypassed | 120 |
| 3b | "A rotor is a wheel of crossed wires" | One rotor swaps each letter for another: a simple substitution, as on the earlier substitution-cipher pages. Name the part as it highlights | One rotor isolated and exploded | 130 |
| 3c | "The rotor turns after every letter" | Stepping means each key press uses a different alphabet; three rotors turn like an odometer. This answers the first Try it observation. Half-finished example: the reader predicts the next lamp | Three rotors, stepping animated on key press | 140 |
| 3d | "The reflector sends the signal back" | The signal goes through the rotors, bounces and returns by another path. Two consequences: the same setting deciphers as well as enciphers, and no letter can ever become itself. This answers the second observation, and plants the weakness | Full there-and-back path lit | 140 |
| 3e | "The plugboard swaps letters in pairs" | Cables on the front swap letter pairs before and after the rotors, multiplying the number of settings. Exact counts go in Dig deeper | Plugboard view, one cable added by tap | 120 |
| 4 | The story: "An army that trusted its machine" | Who built it, who adopted it and why, and how daily keys and per-message keys were used in practice. Keep only what sets up the break. More history (collapsed): commercial origins, variants, naval machines | Model, key-setting view | 170 |
| 5 | How it was broken: "The machine's own rules gave it away" | Prediction: "Here is a ciphertext and a guessed word. Where can the word not sit?" The reader slides the guess along and rules out every position where a letter would match itself. Then the method: operator habits and repeated message keys gave the first way in, and guessed words plus machines that tested settings did the rest. People and dates from the book | Crib-sliding view, clashes marked | 250 |
| 6 | Challenge: "Read an intercepted message" | Easy: decipher with the full setting given. Medium: one rotor position missing. Hard: use a guessed word to find where it fits. Then three recall questions (What changes after each key press? Why can A never become A? What does the plugboard add?). Feedback replays the signal path | Sandbox, all settings open | 70 |
| 7 | What came next: "A secret that had to be shared was the real weakness" | Why the break mattered; the lesson that mechanical complexity did not save a system whose keys had to be distributed and whose users were human; link to the next page in the chronology | Model at rest | 110 |
| 8 | Further reading | Singh's chapters on Enigma; one museum reference such as Crypto Museum's Enigma I page; one place to see a real machine. Collapsed: full references and wiring tables | None | List |

The outline shows the template's main mechanic at work: the two surprises from "Try it" become the questions that segments 3c and 3d answer, and the second answer becomes the tool the reader uses in "How it was broken". The page teaches one chain of ideas, and the challenge is the same activity as the lesson. A Caesar page uses the identical frame with two "How it works" segments and a frequency-count step in section 5.

## The UI rules that make the template work on a phone

**The panel has three states on a phone and is open on desktop.** NN/g says bottom sheets suit cases where users need the background while using the sheet, and should not carry lengthy content, which invites accidental swipe-dismissal ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/bottom-sheet/)). Apple likewise says to consider alternatives to sheets for prolonged flows, and recommends a medium detent on iPhone for progressive disclosure, with a grabber ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sheets)). The reconciliation is a peek state (standfirst, current caption, "Read the story"), a medium state at about half height (one segment at a time, simulator still usable above it), and a large state that behaves like a page: its own scroll, a visible Close button, no swipe-dismiss from mid-article. Expanding pushes a history entry so the browser Back action collapses the sheet, which satisfies NN/g's Back guideline without an in-sheet "Back" label that would contradict Apple's. Only one sheet ever opens; glossary terms expand inline. On desktop the panel sits beside the simulator, open by default and collapsible, because NN/g's accordion guidance says not to hide content users are expected to read when space allows ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/accordions-complex-content/)). Cap the article column at about 65 characters, within Baymard's 50 to 75 convention ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/line-length-readability)).

**Text and simulator stay together, linked by taps, never by hijacked scroll.** Placing words near the matching part of a graphic is one of the best-supported multimedia principles, with reported effects of g = 0.63 in an independent meta-analysis ([catalogue record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi...........04cb527aa23bcab8284e6ebdd56359d5)). So labels go on the model itself, and the reader never scrolls between a diagram and its explanation. The link between article and simulator is an explicit "Show me" control in each segment. A 240-participant study found stepper and scroller navigation did not differ in engagement ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/visual-narrative-flow-exploring-factors-shaping-data-visualization-story-reading-experiences/)), while NN/g found scrolljacking disorienting, worst with text that must be read and worse again on mobile ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/)). Native scroll speed and direction are never altered.

**The stage is a fixed region, so one finger always belongs to the canvas.** A one-finger drag cannot both rotate a model and scroll a page; Google's embedded maps had to adopt two-finger panning plus a fullscreen control to cope ([Google Maps Platform](https://mapsplatform.google.com/resources/blog/smart-scrolling-comes-to-mobile-web-maps/)). Keeping the stage out of the scrolling flow avoids the trap: the page behind it never scrolls, and the article scrolls inside its panel. A slim top bar with the title and a link to the index stays visible in every state as the escape hatch that NN/g found reduces disorientation.

**Accessibility rules are part of the template, not a later pass.** Every drag needs a single-tap alternative under WCAG 2.5.7: preset camera buttons for the model, plus and minus buttons for rotors, and a sheet handle that toggles on tap; keyboard support alone does not satisfy it ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/dragging-movements.html)). Targets are at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels, the AA floor ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html)), with 44 by 44 as the house standard for primary controls. The peek and medium states and the desktop panel are nonmodal, so they use a disclosure button with `aria-expanded` ([APG](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/disclosure/)); only a full-height state that truly blocks the stage becomes a modal dialog, since APG warns against marking reachable content as modal ([APG](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/dialog-modal/)). The WebGL canvas is opaque to assistive technology, so input, output and state appear as real text and every function has an HTML control. Text meets 4.5:1 contrast, the panel reflows at 320 CSS pixels, orientation is not locked, and any auto-rotation longer than five seconds has a pause control ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)). Honour `prefers-reduced-motion` as well; it is cheap, though only AAA.

**Progress is informational, not competitive.** Gamification meta-analyses show small positive averages, around g = .49 for cognitive outcomes ([Sailer and Homner 2020](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056)), but a programming-education meta-analysis found badges, points and leaderboards had no significant effect ([ISCTE](https://repositorio.iscte-iul.pt/bitstream/10071/29213/1/article_95533.pdf)). The template uses a section progress indicator, the three-step challenge ladder, and completion notes that describe a skill ("You deciphered a three-rotor message"). It has no points, streaks, timers, leaderboards or accounts, which also suits a static site.

Two further rules follow from the survey of existing sites. The article text ships as real HTML: CrypTool-Online's Caesar page, fetched without JavaScript, yielded about seven words ([CrypTool-Online](https://www.cryptool.org/en/cto/caesar/)). And headings are meaningful and visually distinct, because NN/g's eyetracking work calls the heading-led "layer-cake" pattern "by far the most effective way" users scan ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/)).

## What remains uncertain

**No study tests this design directly.** The learning effects come from classrooms, labs and MOOCs where learners cannot leave; the notes found no controlled study of self-directed readers on free websites, none comparing story-first with interactive-first ordering, and none of a pinned 3D canvas with sheet-based text on phones. The section order is inferred from six exemplars across two genres, and several likely comparators (Brilliant, The Pudding, 3Blue1Brown, Bletchley Park, wiring-view Enigma simulators) could not be analysed.

**The word counts are house rules.** No peer-reviewed optimum exists for chunk length or page length in self-paced web reading. The V&A limits were written for people standing in galleries. The grade-8 target rests on government convention and one NN/g study, and readability formulas will penalise unavoidable vocabulary such as "polyalphabetic", so apply the target to the surrounding prose.

**Several headline numbers are softer than they look.** The 85% figure comes from a couple of 2015 news graphics reported in blog posts, and Aisch himself stressed that "15% of readers aren't nobody". The spatial-contiguity and several other effect sizes were taken from abstracts or secondary summaries and should be checked before being quoted in a public standard. The narrative advantage loses statistical significance when content is matched across genres.

**Three design tensions need testing with real readers.** The first is the phone layout. A long article in a sheet is what NN/g and Apple advise against, and the three-state design is a reasoned workaround, not a tested one. The strongest alternative is to put the article in normal page flow below the simulator on phones and use the sheet only for step captions. That is simpler and closer to how every exemplar works, but it lets the simulator scroll away from the text and reintroduces the gesture conflict. A small test of the two on the Enigma page is the most valuable next step. The second is coherence against charm: seductive-details research measures immediate learning, and says nothing about whether trimming colourful history reduces enjoyment and return visits, which matter on a hobby site. The "More history" block is the hedge. The third is the challenge: nothing found shows that general readers on phones complete end-of-page challenges, so it must stay optional and its completion rate should be measured.

## Conclusion

The research changes the picture of what the article is for. The brief describes a simulator paired with an article in a panel, which invites treating the article as the content and the simulator as the illustration. The evidence points the other way: for most visitors the stage is the page, so the story has to be legible from the standfirst, the default state and the captions, and the article becomes the layer for readers who choose to go deeper. Writing the eight captions first, and checking that they alone tell the cipher's story, is the most useful discipline the template imposes.

The second implication is economic. A fixed order, fixed labels, one reusable simulator shell with presets, and one caption track mean each of the roughly thirty pages is mostly a writing job, not an engineering job, which is the only way a free static site keeps working across devices for years. Build Enigma first, since it stresses every part of the template, then confirm with Caesar that the frame shrinks gracefully. Instrument three numbers from day one: how many phone readers open the panel, how far they read, and how many attempt the challenge. Those figures will settle most of the open questions above.
