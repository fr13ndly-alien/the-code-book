## The last page is a dare

Most books end with an answer. *The Code Book* ends with a dare.

After the final chapter, Simon Singh prints four cryptograms. Each is meant to be tougher than the one before. Then he warns you that the solutions are not listed anywhere. All you have is your "ingenuity and determination".

This page does the same. The four cryptograms here are ours, not the book's. We wrote the messages, and the ciphers from the earlier pages scrambled them. The book still has its own four, and you can work on those here too.

You have met every cipher used here. Can you break them when nobody gives you the key?

## Three ciphers, three tools

### Caesar: try every key

A Caesar shift has only 25 keys, so you can simply try them all. Cryptogram 2 is the same cipher made harder. It is short, and its spaces are gone. The letters arrive in groups of five, so no little words give it away.

<Try action="second" button="Load cryptogram 2">
  Open "Try every shift at once" on the board. Read down the list for a line that begins with a real word, then tap it. Stuck? Go back to [the Caesar shift](/ciphers/caesar).
</Try>

### Substitution: count the letters

Cryptogram 3 swaps each letter for another, using a shuffled alphabet. It has far too many keys to try. But every plain letter always turns into the same cipher letter, so the habits of English show through.

The solid bars count the cipher letters, tallest first. The outlines show normal English.

<Try action="third" button="Load cryptogram 3">
  Tap the tallest bar, `A`, then tap `e` in the row of plain letters. Next find the three-letter word that keeps coming back. Guess it, and carry on word by word. Stuck? Go back to [frequency analysis](/ciphers/frequency-analysis).
</Try>

### Vigenère: measure the keyword

Cryptogram 4 uses a keyword, so one plain letter becomes several different cipher letters. A simple count tells you nothing.

Look for groups of letters that appear twice instead. The keyword must fit exactly into the gap between the two copies. Once you know its length, each key letter is only a Caesar shift.

<Try action="fourth" button="Load cryptogram 4">
  Press ▲ next to "Key length" until one column of the table is ticked all the way down. Then press ▼ next to "Shift" until the solid bars fit the outlines. Move to the next "Key letter" and do it again. Stuck? Go back to [Babbage's attack](/ciphers/babbage).
</Try>

<Check question="Why must you find the key length before you count any letters?">
  Counting only works on letters that share one cipher alphabet. Until you know the length, you cannot tell which letters belong together.
</Check>

## Ten stages and £10,000

This site follows the shorter edition of the book. The full edition came out in September 1999, and it ended with something bigger: the **Cipher Challenge**.

It had ten stages, and Singh offered £10,000 to the first person to solve them all. The stages followed the history in the book. Early ones used old ciphers, such as a Caesar shift and a Vigenère cipher. Stage 8 was an Enigma message. Stage 10 used RSA with a 512-bit key.

It took more than a year. The winners were five researchers from Sweden: Fredrik Almgren, Gunnar Andersson, Torbjörn Granlund, Lars Ivansson and Staffan Ulfberg. Their claim arrived by post on 7 October 2000. They had broken the last stage without a supercomputer.

<DigDeeper title="A prize for being ahead">

## Breaking a message you know nothing about

On this page we told you each kind of cipher. A real codebreaker has to work that out first. Three habits do most of the work.

**Guess the cipher.** Count the letters. If a few stand tall and others hardly appear, as in English, each letter was probably swapped for one other. If the bars are nearly level, several alphabets are taking turns, as in a Vigenère cipher.

**Count.** In a swap cipher, the tall bars are probably `E`, `T` and `A`. Treat every match as a guess, not a fact.

**Look for patterns.** Short words, doubled letters and repeated groups all survive enciphering. A group that repeats in a Vigenère message measures the keyword. A three-letter word that keeps returning is probably "the".

Then test each guess against the words around it. A wrong guess soon makes nonsense, and you rub it out.
