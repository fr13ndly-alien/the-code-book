## A Friday afternoon in September

Genevieve Grotjan had wanted to be a maths teacher. No school had a job for her. So in 1939 she joined a small team of US Army codebreakers in Washington instead.

For eighteen months the team stared at telegrams between Tokyo and Japan's embassies. A new machine had scrambled them, and no American had ever seen it. They called it **Purple**.

On Friday 20 September 1940, Grotjan, aged 27, was comparing rows of letters on her worksheets. She saw something repeat. Then she saw it again, at the same spacing.

What kind of machine leaves a pattern like that?

<Label>How it works</Label>

## Two machines in one box

### A plugboard splits the alphabet

Purple had no rotors. You typed on an electric typewriter, and a second typewriter printed the result. Between them sat a **plugboard**, a panel of sockets and cables.

The plugboard split the alphabet into two unequal groups. Six letters, the **sixes**, went one way. The other twenty, the **twenties**, went another. The clerks could pick any six letters, and they changed the plugs every day.

<Try action="start" button="Load a real phrase">
  Tap letters on the strip. A brass line under a letter marks a six. Watch which group lights up on the plugboard.
</Try>

### Six letters, one switch

Each group met a **stepping switch**, a part taken from automatic telephone exchanges. Think of an arm that clicks round 25 sets of contacts. Each set is wired differently.

The sixes went through one switch. Each of its 25 positions shuffled the six wires. After every letter the arm clicked on one place. After 25 letters it was back at the start.

<Try action="sixes" button="Follow a vowel">
  The table shows this moment's shuffle of the six. Press ▲ next to "Sixes switch" and watch the bottom row change. Twenty-five presses bring it home.
</Try>

### Twenty letters, three switches

The twenties had a harder road: three switches in a row, each with 25 positions. Together they made 25 × 25 × 25 = 15,625 shuffles of the twenty letters.

Only one of the three stepped after each letter. Usually it was the **fast** switch. When the sixes switch stood on its last position, the **medium** switch stepped instead. Once in 625 letters, the **slow** switch took a turn. The clerks chose which switch played which part.

<Try action="stepping" button="Catch all three stepping">
  Each switch says "steps next" or "stays". Press ▲ next to "Letter shown" four times. You will see fast, then slow, then medium, then fast again.
</Try>

To decipher, the receiver set the same plugs and switch positions and ran the current the other way.

<Try action="start" button="Load the phrase again">
  Press "Feed the output back in". The page switches to "Decipher", and the plain message returns.
</Try>

<Check question="How many shuffles did a vowel meet, and how many did a consonant meet?">
  With the vowels as the sixes, a vowel met only 25 shuffles of six letters, round and round. A consonant met 15,625 shuffles of twenty. One half of the machine was far weaker than the other.
</Check>

<Label>The story</Label>

## The diplomats' machine

Purple belonged to Japan's Foreign Office. From 1939 it carried the most secret telegrams between Tokyo and its ambassadors. It replaced an older machine, which the Americans called Red. Red had also treated the vowels `AEIOUY` apart from the other letters.

The US Army's Signal Intelligence Service set out to break it. William Friedman led the team, and Frank Rowlett was one of its leading members. The secrets it produced were called **Magic**.

The Code Book names Purple in the same breath as the victory at Midway in June 1942. They were different systems. Purple was for diplomats. The Midway orders went out in a naval code, JN-25, which US Navy codebreakers broke.

<Label>How it was broken</Label>

## Count, split, then solve

The attack began with counting. In English the six vowels do about four tenths of all the work. The machine spreads that evenly over six cipher letters. The other twenty letters share what is left, so their bars are about half as tall.

<Try action="counted" button="Count a long ciphertext">
  This is the same real message, enciphered here with the vowels as the sixes. Six bars stand tall. Pick "Sixes first" under "Order of the bars" to check your eye.
</Try>

Once the six were known, the codebreakers could lift them out and study them alone. Six letters and 25 shuffles is a small puzzle. The team had solved it by April 1939.

The twenties took far longer. Grotjan's repeats were the key: they showed that the twenties ran in cycles too. From that the team worked out the three switches and their wiring. By 27 September 1940 they had read two more messages.

Leo Rosen, an engineer on the team, built a copy of the machine from stepping switches. At the end of the war, a piece of a real Purple machine was found in Japan's embassy in Berlin. Its switches were almost the same as the ones Rosen had picked.

</Try>

<Label>What came next</Label>

## No machine at all

Purple's designers kept a habit from the older machine, the split into six and twenty. The habit gave the new machine away.

Cipher machines had another fault on a battlefield: they were slow. Every message had to be typed in and noted down letter by letter, at both ends. Soldiers under fire could not wait.

In 1942 the US Marines tried something with no switches, no wiring and no code books. It was a language almost no outsider could speak: [the Navajo code](/ciphers/navajo).
