## Two discs and a needle

In the fifteenth century an Italian architect, Leon Battista Alberti, sat down with two discs of copper. One was a little larger than the other. Around the edge of each he wrote the alphabet.

He laid the small disc on the large one and pushed a needle through the middle of both. Now the inner alphabet could turn while the outer one stayed still.

It does not look like much. Yet armies used discs like it for five hundred years, and soldiers in the American Civil War still carried them. In the story of secret writing, it is the first machine.

What can two rings of letters do that a pencil cannot?

## One wheel, many alphabets

### Outside plain, inside cipher

The outer ring is the **plain alphabet**: the letters of your message. The inner ring is the **cipher alphabet**: the letters you send.

To encipher, find each letter of your message on the outer ring and write down the inner letter beside it. To decipher, do the reverse: find the letter on the inner ring and read the outer one.

Left still, the disc is a [Caesar shift](/ciphers/caesar). Put the inner `B` beside `A` for a shift of one, or the inner `F` for a shift of five. The disc adds no secrecy to Caesar's cipher. It adds speed, and it stops you miscounting.

<Try action="five" button="Set the disc to F">
  Type your name in "Your message". Tap any letter on the inner ring to bring it round beside `A`, and watch the ciphertext change.
</Try>

### Turn it as you go

Alberti saw that the disc could do more. What if you turn it during the message?

Then the cipher alphabet changes from one letter to the next. The same plain letter gets a different disguise each time. A cipher that uses many alphabets is called **polyalphabetic**, and Alberti was one of the first to think of it.

<Try action="turning" button="Make the disc turn by itself">
  The disc now moves one step after every letter. Type `AAAA` slowly. Watch the ring turn, and read the ciphertext.
</Try>

Four identical letters went in and `ABCD` came out. Someone counting letters to find the most common one would learn nothing. Counting only works when a letter keeps its disguise.

### A keyword says where to turn

Sender and receiver must turn their discs in the same way. One step each time is easy to guess, so the book describes a better rule: a keyword.

Take the keyword `LEON`. For the first letter, put the inner `L` beside `A`. For the second, the inner `E`. Then `O`, then `N`, then back to `L`. The book enciphers "goodbye" like this, and its `g` becomes `R`.

<Try action="leon" button="Load the book's example">
  Read the row "Inner letter at A" under the disc: it spells the keyword again and again. Add letters to the message and watch the disc jump.
</Try>

<Check question="You have met this cipher before under another name. Which?">
  It is the [Vigenère cipher](/ciphers/vigenere). The disc does the job of the big square of alphabets. It is quicker to use and it leads to fewer slips.
</Check>

<Label>The story</Label>

## Five centuries of service

Alberti was a man of the Renaissance: an architect, a painter, a poet and a philosopher. He came to ciphers in the 1460s after a talk with a friend who worked for the Pope.

His disc was basic, but it lasted. The picture in the book shows one used by the Confederate army in the American Civil War, four hundred years later.

The book has a useful name for what the disc does. It calls it a **scrambler**: something that takes each plain letter and turns it into another. A scrambler that stays still is weak. A scrambler that changes while you write is much stronger. Keep that idea. The next machine is built from it.

<Label>How it was broken</Label>

## Still a Caesar, still a Vigenère

A machine is only as strong as the cipher inside it.

A still disc has just 25 useful settings. An enemy who captures a message can try every one, a **brute-force attack**. You can do it now.

<Try action="caught" button="Load a captured message">
  The message `BNWM VXAN LXYYNA` came from a disc that stayed still. Press ▲ next to "Inner letter beside A" until the plaintext turns into English.
</Try>

A disc turned by a keyword is harder, but not safe. It is the Vigenère cipher done by wheel, and the keyword comes round again and again. Charles Babbage and Friedrich Kasiski found how to use that repeat. Once you know the keyword's length, each of its letters is a simple Caesar shift. You can follow the attack on [the Babbage page](/ciphers/babbage).

The disc made enciphering quicker. It did not make the cipher any better.

<Label>What came next</Label>

## The disc goes electric

The lesson of the disc is that a scrambler should keep moving. Its weakness is that it moves in a short, simple pattern, and a short pattern repeats.

In 1918 a German inventor, Arthur Scherbius, set out to fix that. His machine was, in the book's words, "an electrical version of Alberti's cipher disc". It wired several discs together and turned them by itself with every key press. The pattern took thousands of letters to repeat. He called it [Enigma](/ciphers/enigma).
