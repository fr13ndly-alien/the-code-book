<Label>The hook</Label>

## The message nobody could fake

On the island of Saipan, a battalion of American marines moved into positions the Japanese had just left. Then shells began to land around them. Other Americans were firing, sure the enemy was still there.

The marines radioed in English: stop, it is us. The shelling went on. Many Japanese soldiers spoke fluent English, so a voice on the radio proved nothing.

Then one more message went out, this time in Navajo. The guns stopped at once. Only a Navajo marine could have sent it, and only another could understand it.

How can a spoken language work as a code that no enemy can read, or even copy?

<Label>How it works</Label>

## Two layers: a language, then a code

### A language as a wall

The first layer is Navajo itself. It belongs to a family of languages with no link to any language of Asia or Europe. One Navajo verb can carry as much as a whole English sentence. Its ending changes with the kind of object: long, bendy, grainy, bundled and more.

In 1942 very few people outside the Navajo nation could speak it. It had no traditional written form either, so there were no books to learn it from.

But a language alone is not enough. If the code talkers had just translated, any captured Navajo speaker could have understood them. So they built a second layer on top.

### Birds for planes, fish for ships

The first Navajo recruits helped to write a **lexicon**: a list of code words for military terms. They mostly picked words from nature. Planes took the names of birds, and ships the names of fish. The full list had 274 words.

So a message was double-locked. You had to know Navajo, and you had to know that a hummingbird was a fighter plane.

### Spelling out the rest

No list can hold every name and place. For those, the code talkers used a **spelling alphabet**. To send a letter, think of an English word that starts with it, then say that word in Navajo. `A` is "ant", which is `Wol-la-chee`.

<Try action="pacific" button="Spell PACIFIC">
  Read the small words under the boxes: pig, ant, cat, ice, fox, ice, cat. Take the first letter of each. Then type your own name in "Your message" and hear it spelled.
</Try>

### Nothing on paper

The code talkers learned the whole lexicon and alphabet by heart in eight weeks. Navajo stories and family histories had always been passed on from memory, so this came easily. There was no codebook for the enemy to capture.

It also made the code fast. A cipher machine needed every letter typed in and written down. A code talker simply spoke.

<Check question="Why did the code need an alphabet as well as a lexicon?">
  The lexicon only covers the terms its makers thought of. A new place name or a person's name is not in it. The alphabet can spell any word at all, one letter at a time.
</Check>

<Label>The story</Label>

## An idea from childhood

The idea came from Philip Johnston, an engineer in Los Angeles. He was the son of a missionary and had grown up among the Navajo, speaking their language. Early in 1942 he took his plan to the marines at Camp Elliott, near San Diego.

Two Navajo men gave a demonstration. One put six English messages into Navajo and sent them by radio. The other turned them back into English. Every one was right.

Within four months of Pearl Harbor, twenty-nine Navajos began training. In August the first of them landed with the marines on Guadalcanal. At first the code confused American radio operators, who thought the enemy was on their frequencies. Then a code talker raced a cipher machine and won easily.

In all, 420 Navajos served as code talkers. In the first days on Iwo Jima they sent more than eight hundred messages without one error.

<DigDeeper title="An earlier try, and its flaw">
  In the First World War, eight Choctaw men worked as radio operators for an American company in France. No enemy understood them. But Choctaw had no words for modern military things, so a technical term became a loose description, and the listener might get it wrong. The Navajo lexicon was built to cure exactly this.
</DigDeeper>

<Label>How it was broken</Label>

## It never was

Before the code went to war, the marines tested it. They gave a recording to navy intelligence, the team that had cracked Japan's hardest cipher, Purple. After three weeks the codebreakers were still stuck. They could not even write the sounds down, let alone decode them.

The Japanese did no better. After the war their chief of intelligence, Seizo Arisue, admitted they had got nowhere with the Navajo code.

Try the codebreaker's seat yourself.

<Try action="overheard" button="Listen in to one word">
  Seven spoken words, and no meanings. Do not open them yet. Look for words that repeat. Which two words appear twice?
</Try>

`Tkin` and `Moasi` each come twice. Repeats are the weak spot of the spelling alphabet: it is a substitution cipher, and counting can attack it. But you are reading neat spellings. The enemy heard fast, unfamiliar sounds, and most of each message was whole code words, not spelling.

<DigDeeper title="Closing the gap">
  The Navy's published dictionary of the code shows a later fix. Common letters were given extra words: `A` could be ant, apple or axe. A talker could switch between them, so a spelled word repeated less.
</DigDeeper>

<Label>What came next</Label>

## Honoured late

The code stayed secret after the war, and so did the men who spoke it. It was declassified in 1968. In 1982 the United States named August 14 National Navajo Code Talkers Day.

The lesson is simple. Words mean nothing to someone who does not share your language. The Japanese listeners at least had living speakers on the air. Now imagine a script whose last reader died many centuries ago, with nobody left to ask. Scholars faced exactly that, and treated it as codebreaking: [reading the hieroglyphs](/ciphers/hieroglyphs).
