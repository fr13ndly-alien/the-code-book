## A schoolboy's promise

In 1936 a fourteen-year-old schoolboy, Michael Ventris, went to an exhibition in London about ancient Greece and Crete. The famous archaeologist Sir Arthur Evans was there. He showed the visitors clay tablets that he had dug up on Crete.

Neat scratched signs covered the tablets. Evans had studied them for more than thirty years. Yet he had to admit that nobody could read a single word.

Michael asked whether he had heard that correctly. Then he decided that he would read them himself.

The hieroglyphs had needed the Rosetta stone, with its Greek translation. These tablets had no such help. How do you read an unknown script in an unknown language?

## A grid with no sounds

### Words that change their endings

Alice Kober taught classics at Brooklyn College in New York. In her spare time she studied the tablets sign by sign. She refused to guess at sounds.

She found sets of words like the three on the board. Scholars call them **Kober's triplets**. They proved that the language used **inflection**: its words changed their endings.

The script was clearly a **syllabary**. Each sign stands for a whole syllable, usually a consonant plus a vowel. It has 87 sound signs: too many for an alphabet.

Now join the two ideas. Spanish for "friend" is a-mi-go for a boy and a-mi-ga for a girl. In a syllabary the last signs would differ. Yet both would begin with the same consonant.

<Try action="grid" button="Mark the shared signs for me">
  Two signs are ringed: the third sign of the short word and the third sign of the long words. They come where the endings begin. Find them in the grid below. They sit in one row.
</Try>

### Sorting the signs

Kober died in 1950, aged 43, with her work unfinished. Michael Ventris, by then an architect, built on her method.

He searched the tablets for more signs linked in the same way. Signs that seemed to share a consonant went into one row. Signs that seemed to share a vowel went into one column. He called the result a **grid**.

The grid on the board has rows named C1 to C10 and columns named V1 to V5. Ventris had no idea which consonant C9 was. He only knew that its signs belonged together.

Now learn the sound of one sign, and you learn half the sound of every sign in its row and its column.

<Try>
  Count the signs in column V4 of the grid. There are five. All five end in the same vowel, whatever it is. Remember that column.
</Try>

### Three names from Crete

Early in 1952 Ventris made a guess. Some words appeared only on tablets from Crete. Perhaps they named Cretan places, and place names can last for thousands of years.

Take Amnisos, the harbour of Knossos. A syllabary cannot write a consonant alone, so a scribe might spell it `a-mi-ni-so`: four signs, with the last `s` dropped.

<Try action="places" button="Show the place names">

</Try>

The second word now reads `?o-no-so`, although you never touched it. Its middle sign shares a row with `ni` and a column with `so`. So it must be `no`.

</Check>

<Label>The story</Label>

## Harder than hieroglyphs

Arthur Evans began digging at Knossos on Crete in March 1900. Within two weeks he found a store of clay tablets. Fire had destroyed the palace long ago, and the same fire had baked the tablets hard. That is why they survived.

Evans named the script **Linear B**, because its signs were drawn with plain lines.

Compare this with the hieroglyphs. Champollion had the same text in Greek, and he knew Coptic, a descendant of ancient Egyptian. Here there was no translation, and nobody knew the language.

Evans was sure of one thing: it was not Greek. He was so respected that few scholars dared to disagree. He died in 1941 with the tablets unread. Even Ventris expected a language like Etruscan, once spoken in Italy.

<DigDeeper title="The man who numbered the signs">
  Scribes had different handwriting, so the same sign could look like two. An American scholar, Emmett Bennett, sorted every shape into one fixed list. Only then could anyone count signs reliably. His numbers are still used: the sign `ko` is number 70.
</DigDeeper>

## The answer nobody expected

A third name finished the test. The last word read `??-?i-so`. Ventris tried Tulissos, another town on Crete. Before you do the same, try a wrong answer.

<Try action="tulissos" button="Load the third name">
  The bottom word is chosen. Press "Knossos". The caption says why the grid forbids it. Now press "Tulissos", spelled `tu-ri-so` because the script uses one set of signs for both l and r.
</Try>

That is the power of the grid. A wrong name contradicts it at once. A right name fits, and fills in still more signs.

With enough signs filled in, Ventris tried ordinary words. To his surprise they looked like Greek: a very old Greek, about 500 years older than Homer's poems. On July 1, 1952 he announced it on BBC radio.

One listener was John Chadwick, a Cambridge expert in early Greek who had broken codes during the war. He checked the work, was convinced, and joined Ventris.

Proof came in 1953. The American archaeologist Carl Blegen had dug up fresh tablets at Pylos, on the Greek mainland. He tried Ventris's sounds on one that Ventris had never seen. The result, he wrote, seemed "too good to be true".

<Label>What came next</Label>

## A lock made by time

Ventris and Chadwick wrote a book together, *Documents in Mycenaean Greek*. On September 6, 1956, shortly before it came out, Ventris died in a car crash. He was 34.

The tablets were palace records: lists of animals, pots and people. Still, they pushed the written history of Greek back by centuries.

Nobody had tried to hide anything in Linear B. Time alone had locked it. Ventris opened it with a codebreaker's tools: counting, a grid and a few bold guesses.

The book now returns to the Second World War. There, codebreakers faced a cipher too fast to count by hand, and built a machine to do it: [Colossus and the Lorenz cipher](/ciphers/lorenz).
