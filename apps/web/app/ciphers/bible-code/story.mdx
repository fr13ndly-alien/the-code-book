<Label>The hook</Label>

## A prophecy in the headlines

In 1997 a book called *The Bible Code* made headlines around the world. Its author, Michael Drosnin, claimed that the Bible holds hidden messages.

You could not see them by reading in the usual way. Instead you picked one letter to start from. Then you jumped ahead a fixed number of letters, again and again, and wrote down each letter you landed on. Sometimes those letters spelled a word. Drosnin reported an astonishing number of words found like this, and even whole sentences.

It sounds like a cipher with a secret key. But if you find a word by jumping through a text, did somebody put it there?

## Start, skip, read

### Equal jumps

The proper name for this is an **equidistant letter sequence**: a run of letters that sit the same distance apart. You need two numbers to make one. The **start** is the letter you begin on. The **skip** is how far you jump each time.

The Code Book tries it on one of its own paragraphs. It begins at the `M` of "Michael" and notes every fifth letter. The result starts `MESAHIRT`, which means nothing at all. Most sequences are like that.

<Try action="fifth" button="Take every fifth letter">
  The grid now starts at the first letter of the novel, with a skip of 5. Press ▲ next to "Start at letter" a few times and watch "The letters read". Do you get a word?
</Try>

### Let the machine look

Hunting by hand is slow, so let the simulator search. It tries every start with every skip, reading both forwards and backwards. For a four-letter word in this short passage, that makes 259,602 sequences.

<Try action="shot" button="Search for SHOT">
  The controls say how many times `SHOT` hides in the passage. Press ▲ next to "Hit" to see each one lit on the grid. Then type a short word of your own in "Word to find".
</Try>

Three-letter words turn up by the dozen. Four-letter words turn up a few times each. Longer words are rarer, but a longer text gives the search far more places to look.

<Check question="Why are short words easier to find than long ones?">
  Every letter of the word has to land right by luck. A three-letter word needs three lucky landings, and a six-letter word needs six. So long words need many more tries, and a huge text supplies them.
</Check>

<Label>The story</Label>

## An old hunt, a new book

Looking for secrets inside the Bible is an old habit. In the Dark Ages, Europe knew almost nothing about ciphers. The only places that encouraged the study of secret writing were the monasteries, where monks combed the Bible for hidden meanings.

Drosnin's book carried that fascination into modern times. Its method needs no key from the past and no instructions from the author. You choose where to start and how far to jump.

That freedom is the problem, said the sceptics. The Bible is very long. Change both the start and the skip, and a long text offers a vast number of sequences. Some of them are bound to spell something.

<Label>How it was broken</Label>

## The whale that predicted murders

Brendan McKay, of the Australian National University, put the idea to the test. He did not argue about the Bible. He ran the same kind of search on *Moby-Dick*, a novel about hunting a whale.

He found thirteen statements about the assassinations of famous people. Trotsky, Gandhi and Robert Kennedy were among them. Nobody believes the novel foresaw those deaths. So the method "finds" messages wherever it is pointed, and a find proves nothing.

Notice who is doing the work. The searcher picks the word, the start and the skip, and keeps only the results that look meaningful. Thousands of nonsense sequences are thrown away without a mention.

## A real secret has a sender

A real cipher works the other way round. The sender chooses a method and a key before writing. The receiver uses that same key and gets one message, not whichever message suits them.

While monks searched for codes that were not there, real ones were on the way. By the fifteenth century, the book says, European cryptography was "a growing industry", fed by plots between rival courts. Soon a queen would trust her life to a cipher.
