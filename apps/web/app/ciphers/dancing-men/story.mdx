## A child's drawing that frightens a grown woman

Hilton Cubitt, a gentleman from Norfolk, lays a sheet of paper on Sherlock Holmes's table. On it is a row of little stick men. Some wave their arms. Some kick a leg. A few hold flags.

Dr Watson laughs. It looks like a child's scribble. But Cubitt is not laughing. Figures like these keep appearing at his house, chalked on a door and a window sill. Each time, his wife Elsie turns pale with fear, and she will not say why.

Holmes studies the paper for a long time. He is sure the dancers are writing. How can a line of stick men spell a message?

## A stick man for every letter

### One pose, one letter

Each letter of the alphabet has its own dancer. The pose is what matters: where the two arms and two legs point. The same letter always gets the same pose. This is a **substitution cipher**: every letter is swapped for a symbol, and the list of swaps is the **key**.

The key under the message shows all 26 poses. Be warned: they are our own invention. Conan Doyle's messages needed only 18 letters, so his story has no dancer for the other eight. We drew a fresh set in the same spirit.

### Flags instead of spaces

There are no gaps between the dancers. Instead, the last dancer of a word holds a little flag. The flag changes nothing else: an `E` with a flag is still an `E`.

<Try action="trap" button="Load COME HERE AT ONCE">
  Count the flags. There are three, one at the end of each word except the last. The message stops there, so the last word needs none.
</Try>

### Verne's two locks

Jules Verne built a different puzzle into *Journey to the Center of the Earth*. His heroes find a parchment covered in runes. First they swap each rune for an ordinary letter. The result is still nonsense. It only makes sense when they read the letters backwards.

So Verne used two steps: a substitution, then a reversal. To undo them, go in the opposite order.

## When ciphers became entertainment

In the 1800s ordinary people grew fascinated by secret writing, and authors noticed. Verne's novel appeared in 1864. In 1885 he built another, *Mathias Sandorff*, around a cipher.

In America, Edgar Allan Poe dared the readers of a Philadelphia paper to send him ciphers in which each letter was swapped for one symbol. Hundreds arrived, and he solved them all. In 1843 he wrote "The Gold Bug", a story about coded directions to pirate treasure.

In Britain, Arthur Conan Doyle made his detective an expert. Holmes claims to have written a study of "one hundred and sixty separate ciphers". "The Adventure of the Dancing Men" was printed in December 1903.

</DigDeeper>

<Label>How it was broken</Label>

## Holmes counts the dancers

These writers did not cheat. Their heroes break ciphers the way real codebreakers do, with **frequency analysis**: counting the symbols and matching them to the letters of the language.

Holmes begins with the count. In the first message, four dancers out of fifteen are the same. He names that figure `E`. Next he sees that some dancers carry flags, and reasons that a flag ends a word. Now he knows how long each word is.

One message is a single word of five letters, with `E` second and fourth. Holmes reads it as `NEVER`, which gives him `N`, `V` and `R`. Another word must be the wife's name, `ELSIE`. Each new letter unlocks more words. At last Holmes writes a danced message of his own, and the villain walks into the trap.

Legrand does the same in Poe's story. His commonest symbol is an `8`, and he too calls it `E`.

## Hide the habits, not the letters

Stick men, runes and Poe's symbols share one flaw. Each letter always wears the same disguise, so the commonest letter is still the commonest symbol. By the 1800s a detective story could explain the attack, and readers followed every step.

People with real secrets needed a cipher that blurs the count. One answer was to swap letters two at a time, because pairs are far harder to count. Try it on the next page: [the Playfair cipher](/ciphers/playfair).
