<Label>The hook</Label>

## A letter that costs nothing

Picture a young man in Britain in the early 1800s. He has found work far from home, and his mother is waiting for news. A letter costs about a shilling for every hundred miles it travels. He cannot afford that.

But the post office carries newspapers for free. So he takes an old newspaper and a pin. He pushes the pin through the front page a few dozen times, folds the paper, and posts it home.

His mother pays nothing. She unfolds the page and holds it up to the window. A minute later she knows he is well.

How can a pin write a letter on a page that is already full of words?

<Label>How it works</Label>

## One hole for each letter

### Pricking in order

To hide a message, you spell it out with letters that are already on the page. Start at the top. Find the first letter of your secret and prick a hole under it. Then read on from there until you meet your second letter, and prick that.

You never go backwards. The reader will collect the pricked letters from top to bottom, so the holes must come in the same order as your message.

The innocent text that carries the holes is called the **cover text**. Any page will do, as long as it has the letters you need.

<Try action="blank" button="Clear the secret">
  Type a short message in "Your secret". Watch a dot appear under one letter of the newspaper for each letter you type.
</Try>

### When the page runs out

A cover text can only carry what it contains. Our newspaper has just one `Z`, in the word "zest". So it cannot hide a word with two.

There is a second trap. Each hole must come after the one before. A page may hold the letter you want, but too early to be of use.

<Try action="stuck" button="Try to hide PUZZLE">
  Read the caption under the newspaper. It tells you which letters found no place. Now change the secret to `PUZLE` and see it fit.
</Try>

A longer cover text solves both problems. That is why a whole front page worked so well.

### Hidden, not scrambled

Nothing here is scrambled. Every pricked letter is the real letter of the message. The trick is that nobody knows a message is there at all.

Hiding the existence of a message is called **steganography**, from Greek words meaning "covered writing". A cipher is different. It belongs to **cryptography**, which hides the meaning of a message and lets everyone see that it exists.

</Try>

<Label>The story</Label>

## Two thousand years of pins

The idea is ancient. A Greek writer on warfare, Aeneas the Tactician, suggested pricking tiny holes under chosen letters of a harmless text. A guard who glanced at the page would see nothing strange.

About two thousand years later, people in Britain used the same trick for a new reason. They were not hiding from an enemy. They were dodging the cost of the post. Before the postal system was reformed in the middle of the 1800s, a letter cost far more than most people could spare. Newspapers travelled free. A pin and an old front page made a free letter.

By then the public knew a good deal about secret writing. Young couples even sent each other enciphered notes through newspaper advertisements.

<DigDeeper title="Wax, silk and a shaved head">
  Pinpricks are one of many ways to hide a message. The Greek historian Herodotus tells of Demaratus, who warned Sparta of a Persian invasion. He scraped the wax off a pair of writing tablets, wrote on the wood, and covered it with fresh wax.

  Another Greek shaved a messenger's head, wrote on the scalp, and waited for the hair to grow back. In ancient China, messages were written on silk, rolled into a ball, coated in wax and swallowed.
</DigDeeper>

<Label>How it was broken</Label>

## Anyone can hold it to the light

Steganography has one great weakness. If somebody finds the hidden message, they can read it straight away. There is nothing left to work out.

A pricked newspaper passes a quick look. It does not pass a careful search. A suspicious guard only has to do what the true reader does: lift the page to a window. No key is needed and no cleverness.

<Try action="caught" button="Search the newspaper">
  You are the guard. The page is against the light. Read the bright letters from the top down. The secret took you a few seconds.
</Try>

Compare that with a cipher. A guard who catches an enciphered letter knows it is a secret, but still cannot read it. That is why cryptography grew up beside steganography: one hides the message, the other protects it when hiding fails.

Careful people use both. They scramble the message first and hide it afterwards.

## Hide the meaning instead

Pinpricks kept a message cheap and out of sight, but they could not keep it safe from a careful search. For real secrets the Victorians went back to ciphers.

They needed a strong one, because amateur codebreakers were reading the newspapers too. Charles Babbage and his friend Sir Charles Wheatstone enjoyed cracking the enciphered love notes printed there. Wheatstone went on to design a cipher of his own, which swaps letters two at a time. Try it on the next page: [the Playfair cipher](/ciphers/playfair).
