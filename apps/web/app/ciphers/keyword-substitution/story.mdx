<Label>The hook</Label>

## The key in your pocket

Imagine you are a spy with the best cipher of your age. Your cipher alphabet is the 26 letters in a jumbled order that only you and your chief know. No enemy could ever guess it.

But 26 jumbled letters are hard to remember, so you keep them on a scrap of paper. One night a guard searches you and finds the scrap. Now the enemy holds your key. They can read every message you have sent with it.

The cipher was strong. The paper was the weak point. How could you carry a jumbled alphabet in your head instead?

## Any order you like

### A truly jumbled alphabet

The Caesar shift slides the alphabet along, so it has only 25 keys. The general **substitution cipher** removes that limit. Its cipher alphabet may be any rearrangement of the 26 letters.

That gives about 400,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 possible keys. Suppose an enemy checked one key every second. The book reckons the job would take "a billion times the lifetime of the universe". A **brute-force attack**, trying every key, is hopeless.

You use it just like Caesar's cipher. Find each letter of your message in the plain row and write the letter below it.

<Try action="julius" button="Load the message">
  Type your own message in "Your message". Tap under some of its letters and check them against the ciphertext.
</Try>

### Building it from a keyword

A **keyword** or **keyphrase** makes the jumble easy to remember. Take `JULIUS CAESAR`. Remove the space and every repeated letter, which leaves `JULISCAER`. Write that at the start of the cipher alphabet.

Then add the letters you have not used, in their usual order. Begin just after the last letter of the keyphrase. Here that is `R`, so you carry on with `T`, `V`, `W` and so on, and wrap round to `B` at the end.

<Try action="blank" button="Start with no keyword">
  Type a word into "Keyword or keyphrase", one letter at a time. Watch each new letter jump to the front of the cipher row.
</Try>

### Reading it back

Your friend knows the keyphrase, so they can build the same cipher alphabet. To decipher, they find each letter of the ciphertext in the bottom row and read the plain letter above it.

<Try action="reply" button="Load a ciphertext">
  The message `USNJFS HES RISG ZC XJFLE` was made with the book's keyphrase. Read the plaintext in the controls. Then change one letter of the keyphrase and see it fall apart.
</Try>

<Check question="Why is a keyphrase safer than a written-out alphabet?">
  You can keep a phrase in your memory, so there is no paper for an enemy to capture. A keyphrase gives slightly fewer possible keys than a free jumble, but still far too many to try.
</Check>

<Label>The story</Label>

## A thousand years on top

This cipher is simple to use and has a vast number of keys. That mix made substitution the main form of secret writing all through the first millennium AD. Codemakers saw no need to invent anything better.

It was everyday office equipment, too. Officials in the Arab world enciphered affairs of state and even tax records. A tenth-century handbook for secretaries, the *Adab al-Kuttāb*, has sections on how to do it.

Any cipher of this kind, with one cipher alphabet used for the whole message, is called a **monoalphabetic substitution cipher**. The Kama Sutra pairs and the Caesar shift belong to the same family.

<Label>How it was broken</Label>

## Count, don't guess

For centuries scholars believed this cipher could not be broken. They were thinking about the number of keys. But there is a shortcut, and it ignores the keys completely.

One cipher alphabet is used from start to finish. So a plain letter always turns into the same cipher letter. A letter that is common in the message is just as common in the ciphertext, wearing a disguise.

<Try action="caught" button="Load a captured message">
  No key is loaded. Look at "The commonest letter in the ciphertext" in the controls. In English the commonest letter is `E`, so that cipher letter is probably `E`. Now type the keyphrase `JULIUS CAESAR` and tap under plain `E` to check.
</Try>

One letter is only a start. But each letter you unmask helps you guess the words around it. The shortcut was first found by Arab scholars, and it reads a message in minutes, not billions of years.

<Label>What came next</Label>

## The codebreakers arrive

A huge number of keys is not the same as safety. The substitution cipher hides which letter is which, but it cannot hide how often each one appears. Language has habits, and the habits show through.

The people who noticed were scholars in the Arab world. They invented the science of breaking ciphers without the key. Their tool was nothing more than careful counting, and you can use it yourself on the next page: [frequency analysis](/ciphers/frequency-analysis).
