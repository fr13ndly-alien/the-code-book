## The dentist's dare

In 1854 a dentist from Bristol, John Hall Brock Thwaites, wrote to a journal about a new cipher he had invented. He hoped to patent it.

Charles Babbage read the letter and wrote in too. The cipher was not new at all, he said. It was very old, and you could find it in most books. Thwaites had reinvented [the Vigenère cipher](/ciphers/vigenere), nearly three hundred years late.

Thwaites did not say sorry. He dared Babbage to break it instead. That was a bold dare. People called this cipher unbreakable, and most codebreakers had given up on it.

Babbage could not resist. Where do you even start on a cipher that hides how often each letter is used?

## From an echo to the keyword

### Why repeats appear

In a Vigenère cipher, the keyword is written over the message again and again. A keyword of five letters can line up with a word in only five ways. So a common word can be enciphered in only five ways.

In a long message, the same word will sooner or later meet the same part of the keyword. Then the same cipher letters come out. Babbage saw that these echoes were his way in.

The **spacing** is the number of letters from the start of one copy to the start of the next. The keyword must fit into the spacing exactly, with nothing left over.

<Try>
  Tap each row of the table in turn. The caption under the ciphertext tells you the spacing. Short repeats of three letters are left out, because those often are accidents.
</Try>

### The spacing gives the length

Take a spacing of 40. A keyword of 2, 4, 5, 8, 10 or 20 letters would fit into it exactly. These numbers are the **factors** of 40. One repeat cannot tell you which is right.

But the keyword has to fit *every* spacing. The table puts a tick under each length that fits a row. You are looking for a column that is ticked all the way down.

<Try>
  Press ▲ next to "Key length" and watch the bottom line of the table. Stop at the length that explains all eight repeats.
</Try>

<Check question="How long is the keyword?">
  Five letters. Every spacing in the table divides by 5: 65, 95, 105, 145, 195, 40, 255 and 305. No other length fits more than three of them.
</Check>

### Five easy ciphers

Now comes the clever part. If the keyword has five letters, then letters 1, 6, 11, 16 and so on were all enciphered with the same key letter. They all used one row of the Vigenère square. One row is just a [Caesar shift](/ciphers/caesar).

So the unbreakable cipher is five Caesar shifts taking turns. And a Caesar shift cannot hide how often letters are used. Pull out every fifth letter, count them, and the shape of English is there. It has only been slid along.

<Try action="letters" button="Go to step 2">
  The solid bars count every fifth letter. The outlines show ordinary English. Press ▼ next to "Shift" eight times, to `S`. The tall bar lands on `E`, and the rare letters `J`, `Q`, `X` and `Z` fall empty.
</Try>

### One letter at a time

The first key letter is `S`. Do the same for the other four. English has a tall `E`, three strong bars at `R`, `S` and `T`, and then a long dip from `U` to `Z`. Slide each chart until those features line up.

## The man who wanted steam to do sums

Charles Babbage was born in 1791, the son of a rich London banker. He is best known for designing calculating machines, the ancestors of the computer. The idea came in 1821, when he found page after page of mistakes in printed tables of numbers. He wished the sums had been "executed by steam".

He had loved ciphers since school. Older boys made up their own, and he worked out the keys. Sometimes they beat him for it.

His attack on the Vigenère cipher needed no machine, only patience and counting. He probably finished it in 1854, soon after the dare.

## A secret for nine years

The weakness is the repeating keyword. It turns one strong cipher into a few weak ones, and the repeats tell you how many.

Guessing the wrong length shows why the first step matters.

<Try action="intercept" button="Start again">
  Set "Key length" to 4. Pick "2 Key letters" and slide the shift. The bars stay flat and lumpy, and no shift looks like English.
</Try>

Babbage never published his method. Nobody knew he had done it until scholars read through his notes in the twentieth century.

Why keep quiet? He often left work unfinished. But there is another possible reason. The Crimean War had just begun, and Britain was fighting Russia. The British army may have asked him to keep the secret.

Meanwhile a retired Prussian army officer, Friedrich Wilhelm Kasiski, found the same method by himself. He published it in 1863. Ever since, hunting for repeats has been called the **Kasiski test**, and Kasiski got the credit.

## Secrets in the newspaper

The lesson is that a key must not repeat. A short keyword used again and again leaves echoes, and echoes can be measured. Later codemakers would try keys as long as the message itself.

By now ciphers were no longer just for kings and generals. The telegraph had made ordinary people think about who might read their messages. Some hid notes in newspapers, in ciphers or in something even simpler: tiny holes. See [the pinprick message](/ciphers/pinprick).
