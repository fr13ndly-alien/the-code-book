<Label>The hook</Label>

## A stone in a wall

In 1799 French soldiers were knocking down an old wall at a fort in Rosetta, a town in the Nile Delta. Built into the wall was a dark slab covered in writing.

The scholars travelling with Napoleon's army saw at once why it mattered. The stone carried one text three times: in Greek, in an Egyptian script called demotic, and in hieroglyphs. Scholars could read the Greek with ease. Nobody on Earth could read the other two.

The last people who understood hieroglyphs had died long before. No teacher, no dictionary and no speaker was left.

How do you read a script when there is no one to ask what a single sign means?

<Label>How it works</Label>

## Names are the way in

### The ring around a king

In the summer of 1814 an English scientist, Thomas Young, took a copy of the Rosetta stone on holiday. He noticed that some groups of hieroglyphs sat inside a loop. Such a loop is called a **cartouche**.

Young guessed that a loop marked something important: the king's name. The Greek text named the king as Ptolemaios. And a name sounds much the same in any language.

That gave Young a **crib**: a piece of the answer known in advance. He matched the signs in the loop to the sounds of the name, in order.

<Try>
  Finish the top ring. Tap each sign in turn and give it the Greek letter printed above it: `t`, `o`, `l`, `m`, `e`, `s`.
</Try>

### A second name tests the first

One name proves little. Perhaps the match is luck. In 1822 a Frenchman, Jean-François Champollion, studied an obelisk carved in Greek and hieroglyphs. It had two cartouches: Ptolemy again, and Queen Cleopatra.

The two names share the sounds p, t, o, l and e. If Young's idea was right, the signs for those sounds in Ptolemy should turn up in the right places in Cleopatra.

<Try action="ptolemy" button="Load Ptolemy, fully read">
  Look at the middle ring. Three sounds have appeared under the right Greek letters: `l`, `o` and `p`. Now give the other signs their Greek letters. The single reed is the `e` of the pair of reeds above.
</Try>

Only t did not match. Ptolemy writes it with a half circle and Cleopatra with a hand. Champollion decided that two signs could share one sound, as `c` and `k` do in "cat" and "kid".

<Check question="Why was the match with Cleopatra such strong evidence?">
  Nobody chose those signs to fit. The lion, the lasso and the square got their sounds from Ptolemy alone. Then they landed exactly on L, O and P in a different name. Luck does not do that three times.
</Check>

<Label>The story</Label>

## Why nobody had tried

Egyptians wrote in hieroglyphs for over three thousand years. Then, near the end of the fourth century AD, the old scripts were banned as pagan and replaced by a new one, Coptic. Within a few lifetimes nobody could read the temple walls.

Later scholars made a wrong guess and stuck to it. They thought each sign was a **semagram**: a picture standing for a whole idea. In 1652 a famous priest, Athanasius Kircher, published "translations" built on that belief. They were pure invention, but he was so respected that people trusted them.

So the Rosetta stone, cut in 196 BC, sat in the British Museum from 1802 with its Greek fully translated. Few people dared to treat the signs as **phonograms**: signs for sounds.

Even Young lost his nerve. He decided that sounds were only used to spell foreign names, such as the Greek Ptolemy, and he gave up.

<DigDeeper title="Three scripts and a picture puzzle">
  Hieroglyphs were slow to draw, so scribes used a quicker everyday script called hieratic. About 600 BC an even simpler one, demotic, took its place. All three wrote the same language, a little like three fonts. Coptic, which replaced them, used 24 Greek letters and six demotic signs.

  You may know the rebus from puzzle books. To write "belief", draw a bee and then a leaf. The pictures stand for sounds, not for insects or trees.
</DigDeeper>

<DigDeeper title="A damaged crib">
  The stone was no easy answer sheet. Half the lines of hieroglyphs are missing, and the rest are broken. Worse, nobody had spoken ancient Egyptian for at least eight centuries. Matching signs to Greek words could give a meaning, but not a sound.
</DigDeeper>

<Label>How it was broken</Label>

## A sun that says "ra"

Champollion read more foreign names, but they proved nothing new. Then, on September 14, 1822, he received drawings from the temple of Abu Simbel. Its cartouches were older than any Greek ruler of Egypt.

One held just four signs. The last two were known: `s` and `s`. The first was a circle like the sun. Champollion had learned Coptic, the last form of the Egyptian language, as a teenager. The Coptic word for sun is "ra".

So the name ran ra, something, s, s. Only one pharaoh fitted: Rameses. The missing sign had to be `m`.

<Try action="rameses" button="Load the Abu Simbel cartouche">
  The bottom ring reads `? ? s s`. The sun sign is chosen. Press `ra`. Then tap the second sign and press `m`.
</Try>

This was the breakthrough. An Egyptian name, far older than the Greeks, was spelled with sounds too. And the sun showed a second trick, the **rebus**: a picture used for the sound of its word. It also pointed to the language behind the signs: Coptic's ancestor. Champollion ran to tell his brother, then collapsed.

<Label>What came next</Label>

## Curiosity, not war

In 1824 Champollion published his full system. He saw Egypt for himself at last, and died in 1832, aged forty-one.

Nobody had hidden anything in the hieroglyphs. Yet reading them took a codebreaker's tools: a crib, a guess, and a second text to test the guess.
