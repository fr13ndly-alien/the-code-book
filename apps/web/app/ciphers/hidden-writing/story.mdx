## A message under the hair

Histaiaeus had a dangerous message to send. He wanted his ally Aristagoras, in the city of Miletus, to rise up against the king of Persia. A letter like that must never be found on the road.

So Histaiaeus picked a messenger and shaved the man's head. He wrote the message on the bare scalp. Then he waited for the hair to grow back.

The messenger set off with nothing in his hands and nothing in his bag. Nobody on the road troubled him. When he reached Miletus he shaved his head again and bowed it towards Aristagoras.

Not one word of that message was scrambled. Anyone who saw it could read it. So what kept it secret?

## Hide the message, not the meaning

### Under the wax

Hiding the fact that a message exists is called **steganography**. The name comes from two Greek words: *steganos*, "covered", and *graphein*, "to write".

Notice what is missing. There is no secret alphabet and nothing to work out. The message sits there in plain words. Its only protection is that nobody thinks to look.

The person at the other end needs just one piece of knowledge: where to look.

### Ink that waits for heat

You can also hide writing in the ink itself. In the first century AD, the Roman writer Pliny the Elder described an invisible ink made from the "milk" of the tithymalus plant. Once dry, it is transparent. The page looks empty.

Gentle heat brings the words back. The dried sap chars and turns brown. Many fluids from living things behave the same way, because they are rich in carbon.

<Try action="letter" button="Write in invisible ink">
  Press "Let the ink dry" and the note vanishes. Now press "Warm the page" once, then again. Watch the letters darken a little with each press.
</Try>

<Check question="What must the receiver know to read a hidden message?">
  Only the trick: scrape the wax, or warm the page. There is no key to remember and no puzzle to solve. That makes hidden writing easy to use, and it is also the reason it is fragile.
</Check>

## The woman who said "scrape it"

In 480 BC a pair of writing tablets reached Sparta from the Persian city of Susa. They were blank. Nobody could think why anyone would send them.

Then Gorgo spoke up. She was the daughter of Cleomenes and the wife of Leonidas. She told the others to scrape off the wax. On the wood underneath was a warning from Demaratus, a Greek living in exile in Susa. Xerxes, king of Persia, had spent five years secretly gathering a vast force to invade Greece.

The Greeks had been defenceless. Now they spent the profits of their silver mines on warships. On 23 September 480 BC they lured the Persian fleet into the narrow Bay of Salamis and beat it in a single day.

We know both stories from Herodotus, the Greek historian who recorded these wars.

## The guard who checks everything

Hidden writing has one great weakness. If the message is found, it is read at once. Nothing stands between the finder and the words.

And finding it takes no cleverness, only patience. Picture a guard at a border who trusts nobody. He scrapes every wax tablet. He holds every blank sheet over a flame. He shaves the head of anyone who looks nervous. Most days he finds nothing. Sooner or later he finds something.

The guards on the road from Susa only looked. A sender cannot count on that. Hiding offers some safety, which is why it lasted so long. But a secret that matters needs a second line of defence.

## Hide the meaning instead

So a second art grew up beside the first. **Cryptography** takes its name from the Greek *kryptos*, "hidden". It does not hide the message. It hides what the message means, by scrambling it in a way that sender and receiver have agreed in advance. This scrambling is called **encryption**.

Now the thorough guard can scrape and heat all he likes. He finds the message, and still cannot read it.

The simplest way to scramble is to keep every letter and change the order. Try it on the next page: [the rail fence](/ciphers/rail-fence).
